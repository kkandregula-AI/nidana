AI Education Series · Episode 13

How a machine reasons about disease — and where the reasoning breaks

Diagnosis is not pattern matching. It is belief revision under uncertainty, in a specific population, with asymmetric costs of being wrong. This episode makes that arithmetic visible, then shows you exactly which parts of it current AI does well, does badly, and cannot do at all.

The one sentence this whole episode defends

A test result does not tell you what a patient has. It tells you how much to change your mind about what they might have.

Everything downstream — why a 99%-accurate model can be wrong 95% of the time, why an imaging model collapses at a new hospital, why a fluent language model invents a drug dose with total confidence — falls out of that sentence.

Chapter one · Pūrva — what was true before the test

A 99% accurate test is usually wrong

This is the single most misread number in medical AI. Accuracy is a property of the test and the population together. Move the population, and the same test changes meaning entirely.

Worked example, by hand

Disease affects 1 in 1,000. Test is 99% sensitive, 99% specific. Screen 100,000 people:

Has diseaseDoes not
Test +99999
Test −198,901
Total10099,900

Of the 1,098 people told "positive", only 99 are ill. Nine percent. The test did nothing wrong. There are simply 999 times more well people available to be wrongly flagged.

Why this ruins AI benchmark claims

A model reports 99% accuracy on a curated test set that was balanced 50/50 between sick and well — because balanced sets train better. Deploy it into a screening clinic where 0.1% are sick and nothing about the model changed, but its positive predictions went from mostly-right to mostly-wrong.

The ruleSensitivity and specificity travel with the model. Positive predictive value travels with the population. Any vendor quoting only the first pair is quoting the half that flatters them.

This is also why the same model must be revalidated in every setting it moves to — a lesson the imaging chapter makes visual.

What this engine is, exactly simulated

It is naive Bayes in log-odds space. Prior odds, times the likelihood ratio of every finding you switched on, normalised across hypotheses. Roughly forty lines of arithmetic — no model, no training, no network.

Chapter three · Dṛś — what the machine sees

Medical imaging AI, drawn pixel by pixel

A radiology model does not "look at a chest". It multiplies small grids of numbers across the image, millions of times, and learns which combinations predict the label it was given. Everything good and everything catastrophic about imaging AI follows from that.

Step one · a convolution is a sliding weighted average

Below is a synthetic radiograph drawn by this app — thorax, ribs, heart shadow, diaphragm, one small nodule in the right upper zone, and a metal side-marker in the corner. Nothing here came from a patient. Choose a 3×3 kernel and watch what survives.

Edit any cell. The kernel is applied to every pixel: multiply the nine neighbours by these nine weights, add them up, that is the new pixel. A trained network learns thousands of these, stacked.

Original — no filter applied.

The idea in one lineEarly layers find edges. Middle layers combine edges into textures and shapes. Late layers combine shapes into "this looks like the thing the label said". At no point does the model know what a lung is.

Chapter four · Sandeha — confidence without knowledge

Calibration, drift, and fabrication

The dangerous model is not the one that is wrong. It is the one that is wrong and sounds exactly as certain as when it is right.

Calibration · does 90% mean 90%?

A calibrated model that says "80% chance" is right about eighty times in a hundred. Most raw neural networks are systematically overconfident. The dashed diagonal is perfect calibration; the curve is the model.

—
Calibration error
—
Behaviour

Temperature scaling divides the model's raw scores by one number before the final softmax. It changes no prediction's rank — accuracy is untouched — but it makes the stated confidence honest. It is one of the cheapest and least-used safety measures in deployed clinical AI.

Now—

Distribution shift · the model does not know it is lost

A classifier assigns probabilities across the classes it was taught. Show it something outside that set and it does not return "unknown" — it returns a confident answer from the wrong menu.

Fabrication Lab constructed examples

Six outputs in the register of a confident clinical assistant. Some are sound. Some contain a specific failure that has caused real harm. Mark each one, then reveal.

Written by the author to be diagnosable. None is a transcript of a real model, and none should be used as clinical information either way.

Why fluency is not evidence

A language model is trained to produce text that a knowledgeable person would plausibly have written. A correct citation and an invented one are equally plausible as text. The model has no separate channel that says "I retrieved this" versus "I generated this" — and the generated one is often more fluent, because it was shaped to fit the sentence rather than to fit the world.

In clinical use the fabrications cluster in exactly the places verification is hardest: reference ranges, dosing in unusual populations, guideline version numbers, trial names, and the confident answer to a question containing a false premise.

The false-premise trapAsk about a syndrome that does not exist and a fluent model will frequently describe it — mechanism, presentation, management — because the question presupposed it. Clinicians embed false premises constantly and without meaning to: "her rash is definitely drug-related, which antibiotic…"
What actually helpsRetrieval with visible sources · refusing outside a defined scope · showing a probability distribution rather than a single answer · a second model checking the first · and a human who is accountable and expected to disagree.
Chapter five · Manuṣya — the part with no dataset

Why AI cannot replace your doctor

Not because it is not clever enough. Because most of what a consultation does never becomes data in the first place — and because someone has to be answerable when it goes wrong.

Read this chapter carefullyThe argument is not "AI is bad at medicine". Several systems in the next chapters are genuinely better than the average human at their narrow task. The argument is that the narrow task is a small slice of the job, and the slice that is left is the slice that decides outcomes.

The same encounter, two ways of seeing it

One patient. Toggle between the record a model receives and the room a clinician is standing in.

Where the responsibility actually sits

QuestionA modelA clinician
Can it be wrong?YesYes
Can it know it is out of its depth?Only if someone built that boundary in — and it will not notice a boundary nobody anticipatedTrained explicitly to escalate, and answerable for failing to
Can it be examined about its reasoning afterwards?Post-hoc explanations are reconstructions, not the actual computationYes, under oath if necessary
Can it hold a licence, be suspended, be sued?No. Liability lands on the deployer, the manufacturer, or the clinician who accepted the outputYes
Can it negotiate what matters to this patient?It can generate options; it cannot be trusted with the trade-offThis is the actual job
Automation bias, the quiet riskThe most reliable finding in decision-support research is that people follow confident machines — including when the machine is wrong, and including experts who were told the machine is sometimes wrong. A tool that is right 95% of the time can still reduce total accuracy, if it makes clinicians stop checking the other 5%.

The honest formulation

AI will not replace your doctor. It will replace a set of tasks your doctor currently does badly, slowly, or not at all — and the doctors who use it well will do things the ones who don't cannot.

The failure mode to guard against is not the machine taking over. It is the machine being trusted in exactly the cases where it should not have been, by someone with no time to check.

Chapter six · Aṅga — organ by organ

Where clinical AI actually works today

A tour of the body, not as anatomy, but as twelve very different reasoning problems. Each organ tells you why some of them fell to machine learning a decade ago and others have not moved at all.

Educational reference onlyThe presentations and tests below are here to explain why each organ is an easy or hard target for AI. They are not a symptom checker, not a diagnostic pathway, and deliberately contain no doses, no thresholds for treatment, and no advice. Anything that worries you belongs in front of a qualified clinician, not an app.

The pattern across all twelve

AI does well when…AI struggles when…
The input is a single standardised image or signalThe input is a person's story over months
The label is objective and verifiable later (biopsy, angiogram, culture)The label is a clinician's opinion, so the model learns the opinion
Millions of labelled examples existThe condition is rare — which is exactly when help is most wanted
The task is triage: find the ones worth looking at firstThe task is deciding whether to treat at all
An expert is scarce and the alternative is no assessmentAn expert is present and the model only adds a second opinion nobody has time to adjudicate
The strongest case for clinical AI is not accuracyIt is access. A model that is merely as good as an average reader is transformative in a district where there is no reader at all, and marginal in a tertiary centre with twelve of them. The eye and the chest are the two clearest demonstrations of this in the world right now.
Chapter seven · Bhaviṣya — the horizon

What AI will plausibly do to healthcare

Sorted by how much evidence exists, not by how exciting it sounds. Four bands: running on patients now, in trials, in the lab, and speculation.

The three things that decide whether any of it helps

Evaluation

Almost all published clinical AI is validated retrospectively on data resembling its training set. The number that matters — did patients do better in a prospective trial — exists for a small handful of systems. Diagnostic accuracy is not a health outcome.

Workflow

Systems fail on integration far more often than on accuracy. An alert that arrives after the decision, in a system nobody has open, addressed to a role that does not exist on nights, is a correct prediction and a clinical nullity.

Equity

Models inherit whoever was in the training data. Skin tone, body habitus, gender, language, and cost-of-care proxies have all produced documented disparities. A model trained where care was rationed learns the rationing as if it were biology.

Governance, brieflyClinical AI is regulated as a medical device in most jurisdictions — meaning it needs evidence, post-market surveillance, and a named responsible party. Continuously-learning models sit awkwardly here, because the thing that was approved is not the thing running next month. The unresolved questions are the same everywhere: who validates locally, who monitors drift, and who is liable when a clinician follows a correct-looking wrong answer.

A realistic ten-year picture

Documentation is largely automated and clinicians look at patients again. Imaging models triage the queue everywhere and read independently in a few narrow, heavily-audited tasks. Screening for eye, chest and cervix reaches populations that had none. Most consultations still begin with a person asking why you came in — because that question, and what you decide to do about the answer, was never the part that was scarce.

Chapter eight · Parīkṣā — check yourself

Ten questions

Every one is answerable from something in this episode. Explanations appear as you go.

Take this with you

Five questions to ask about any clinical AI system anyone tries to sell, deploy, or publish:

  1. What was the prevalence in the validation set, and what is it where you intend to use it?
  2. Was it validated prospectively, externally, at a site that did not build it?
  3. What does it do when the input is outside what it was trained on — and how do you find out that happened?
  4. What is the error you are choosing at this operating point, and who agreed to that trade?
  5. When it is confidently wrong, who is accountable, and how would anyone notice?
If you only remember one thingProbability before the test decides what the test means. Everything else in medical AI is an elaboration of that.
